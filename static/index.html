About

Where this comes from

Every record here is the FCC's, rebuilt weekly from the Media Bureau's AM, FM and TV queries. Nothing is hand-maintained.

What distance does and does not tell you

Stations are ranked by how far you are from the transmitter, which is a reasonable guide on FM and a poor one on AM.

FM travels roughly line-of-sight, so distance, power and antenna height explain most of what you will hear — though a ridge in the way still beats all three.

AM is different. By day it follows the ground, and how far it gets depends on the conductivity of the soil it is crossing. After dark it bounces off the ionosphere, and a station hundreds of miles away can arrive louder than one down the road while that nearby station cuts power or switches to a directional pattern to protect it. Day and night power are both listed for this reason.

What the class letter means

Every station carries a class, and on neither band is it a power rating.

On FM it is a power and height pair. The FCC protects a station out to a fixed distance, so the same reach can be bought with a tall antenna and modest power or a short one and a great deal more. Class C and class C1 stations both run 100 kW; what separates them is that a class C antenna sits more than twice as high above the surrounding terrain. This is why height is listed on a station's page beside its power — on FM the two only mean something together.

On AM the class is not about the transmitter at all. It describes the channel and the protection that comes with it: A is a clear channel, B regional, C a local one, D limited at night. Class B, C and D all reach 50 kW at the top of their range while their middles sit at 5, 1 and 1 kW, so power barely separates them — a local-channel station may run as much power as a regional one and still be class C.

Two things look wrong here and are not. A small number of FM stations are class D, running tens of watts rather than kilowatts: a legacy educational class largely closed to new applicants, which is why they sit beside 100 kW neighbours reading as almost nothing. And a handful of AM records carry no class at all, which is why the legend promises only that a letter may follow the code.

The two numbers on a TV station

A television station is listed here as ch 23 (6). Those are different numbers and only the first one is real.

The first is the RF channel — the actual slice of spectrum the transmitter occupies, and the thing that decides which antenna you need: channels 2 to 6, 7 to 13 and 14 up are three different antennas. The second, in brackets, is what the set puts on screen. That is almost always the station's old analogue channel, carried over through the digital switch and the repack so a station that spent fifty years calling itself Channel 6 did not have to stop.

This is not a curiosity. Hundreds of stations display a number from a different antenna band than they transmit on — WTVR-TV is CBS 6 in Richmond and broadcasts on RF 23, which is UHF. Buy an antenna for what the screen says and you will not receive it. The TV band groups by the RF channel for exactly this reason.

The display number is missing altogether from most of the transmitters listed, nearly all of them low power: the major channel is assigned with a full power licence and most low power stations have none. Those show the RF channel alone, which is the one that always exists.

What the TV service codes mean

Television has no class letter. The service code is the class, and it says what kind of thing the transmitter is — which on this band matters more than it does on radio, because half of what is listed relays somebody else rather than being a station in its own right.

DTV is full power, and it is what most people mean by a television station: up to 1,000 kW, a network on file, and a display channel the set shows. DCA is Class A — low power stations that earned protected status in 1999 by meeting programming obligations, and so cannot be pushed off their channel the way ordinary low power can. They carry -CD call signs.

The rest relay. LPT is a television translator, picking up a station and repeating it on another channel, and it is the largest group here. LPD is low power digital that originates its own programming, under an -LD call sign, though in practice many carry a network's feed. DRT is a replacement translator, licensed to a full power station to give back coverage it lost moving channels in the digital transition — which is why those carry their parent's call sign rather than one of their own.

DTS is the odd one. A distributed transmission system is one station broadcasting from several synchronised transmitters on the same channel, so a market that one mast cannot cover is filled by three or six. They share a call sign and a licence and differ only in where they stand, which is exactly what makes each of them worth listing separately.

Translators, boosters and why one station has several entries

A translator picks up a station and repeats it on a different frequency, which is why a small town can carry a city station down at the far end of the dial. It has its own call sign and its own place on the dial, so it is listed separately here — which is where you tune for it.

A booster repeats its parent on the same frequency, filling a valley or a downtown shadow the main transmitter cannot reach. It is listed separately too, since its transmitter is somewhere else and that is the whole reason you can hear the station where you are. But it is not a separate station to listen to: it carries a derived call sign like KHPR-FM1 and identifies on air as its parent. Two entries on one frequency close together are usually a station and its booster, not two stations.

Television does the same thing under its own names, and further. A distributed transmission system runs several synchronised transmitters on one channel, and a replacement translator fills in what a station lost when it changed channel. Each is a separate transmitter in a separate place, so each is listed separately — KCWX near San Antonio is one licence across six of them, and KNME-TV in Albuquerque runs two translators sixty miles apart alongside its main transmitter. Hundreds of facilities here hold more than one live transmitter, and listing a single one of them would hide the one you can actually receive.

Licensed is not the same as on the air

A station may stay silent for up to twelve months and keep its licence. The FCC's data does not mark it, and neither can this — a station listed here may have been dark for months.

Across the borders

Canadian and Mexican FM stations appear, because the FCC records them under the coordination agreements that keep each side from interfering with the others. That coverage reaches a couple of hundred miles inland and no further, so a Vancouver or Monterrey listener is served and a Winnipeg or Mexico City one is not.

AM reaches much further. Medium wave crosses the hemisphere after dark, so every country in the Americas notifies its assignments and the FCC files all of them — some seven thousand stations from Mexico, Brazil, Cuba, Colombia and beyond. They are listed here. By day they are irrelevant to you; on a winter night they are the whole point.

The tables abbreviate the country to two letters to keep the column narrow. Tap a station for the country spelled out — which is worth doing, because these are the FCC's own abbreviations and not the ISO codes they resemble. Several mean a different country than you would guess: PA is Paraguay while Panama is PM, ES is El Salvador rather than Spain, CI is Chile, NA is the Netherlands Antilles, and VI is the British Virgin Islands. Four more — BL, BD, BF and ST — are Bolivia, Bermuda, the Bahamas and Saint Lucia. The station page always has the full name, so the safe habit is to look rather than to translate.

Networks, and what a network here means

Television stations carry the network they are affiliated with — NBC against WWBT, and Independent where that is the answer. It comes from the FCC's Licensing and Management System rather than from the station queries the rest of this is built on, joined on the facility number both of them carry, so it is an exact match and not a guess made from a licensee reading "Nexstar Media Inc."

Two things to know about it. It is licence data, so it says what a station has told the FCC it carries, not what is on the air tonight — an affiliation that changed last week may not have been filed yet. And it is recorded for essentially all full power television and almost no low power, which is why most translators show nothing: the FCC has nothing on file for them, rather than this having failed to look.

The same source marks the stations broadcasting ATSC 3.0, the newer standard sold as NextGen TV. Those carry a 3.0 tag beside their network. It needs a tuner built for it: a set bought before about 2020 cannot decode it at all.

The tag does not mean you have lost the station. A station broadcasting 3.0 is currently still required to keep an ordinary ATSC 1.0 simulcast running, usually from a neighbouring transmitter, so an older set goes on receiving the programming. What the tag says is that this transmitter carries 3.0 — it does not say where the 1.0 copy is, because the FCC does not file that here. The FCC has now ordered every full power station onto 3.0, and the simulcast requirement ends with it, so expect both the tag to spread and that reassurance to expire.

HD Radio, and the HD tag

The same source records whether a radio station transmits digitally. Stations running hybrid — analog and digital together, which is what HD Radio is — carry an HD tag beside their service code. About 2,200 full power FM stations do, and rather fewer AM.

It is in-band on-channel: the digital signal is not somewhere else on the dial, it sits in the sidebands of the same channel, from the same transmitter. The offset is fixed by the standard rather than chosen by the station — roughly 129 to 198 kHz either side of centre, on both sides at once. A hybrid station on 99.1 therefore puts digital energy at about 98.90–98.97 and 99.23–99.30 MHz.

Which is where the first adjacent channels live, and why the tag is worth reading on a neighbour rather than on the station you want. If 89.1 is a mess, an HD tag on 89.3 is a large part of the answer. What it cannot tell you is how bad: the FCC permits a tenfold range of digital power and does not file which a station uses. On AM the geometry is worse still — sidebands reach about ±15 kHz on a 10 kHz channel spacing, overlapping both neighbours by design.

A very few stations are all-digital, with no analog signal at all, and are tagged HD only. An ordinary radio tuned to one hears nothing. There are two on AM and none on FM.

A station with no tag is not necessarily analog. The FCC records analog explicitly, but leaves the field blank on a great many licences — 45% of FM and 78% of AM — so an absent tag means the licence does not say.

What is missing

Formats, on radio. The FCC licenses transmitters rather than programmes, so nothing here knows which station is country and which is news — and format is the thing about a station most likely to change.

Television is the exception, and only a partial one. The network is on file and is shown, but nothing past it is: no schedule, nothing about what is on now, and no sub-channels. 12.1 and 12.2 are decided in the broadcast stream rather than in a licence, so a set that finds dozens of channels is finding a handful of transmitters. Nor does anything here know which ATSC 3.0 broadcasts are encrypted, and some are. RabbitEars tracks both, by hand, and is where to go for them.
