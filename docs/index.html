About

Where this comes from

Every record here is the FCC's, pulled from the Media Bureau's AM and FM queries and rebuilt weekly. Nothing is hand-maintained.

What distance does and does not tell you

Stations are ranked by how far you are from the transmitter, which is a reasonable guide on FM and a poor one on AM.

FM travels roughly line-of-sight, so distance, power and antenna height between them explain most of what you will hear — though a ridge in the way still beats all three.

AM is different. By day it follows the ground, and how far it gets depends on the conductivity of the soil it is crossing. After dark it bounces off the ionosphere, and a station hundreds of miles away can arrive louder than one down the road while that nearby station cuts power or switches to a directional pattern to protect it. Day and night power are both listed for this reason.

What the class letter means

Every station carries a class, and on neither band is it a power rating.

On FM it is a power and height pair. The FCC protects a station out to a fixed distance, so the same reach can be bought with a tall antenna and modest power or a low one and a great deal of it. 453 class C stations run 100 kW from a median 457 m above the surrounding terrain; 827 class C1 stations run that same 100 kW from 189 m. Identical transmitter power, different class, different coverage. This is why height is listed on a station's page beside its power — on FM the two only mean something together.

On AM the class is not about the transmitter at all. It describes the channel and the protection that comes with it: A is a clear channel, B regional, C a local one, D limited at night. Class B, C and D all reach 50 kW at the top of their range while their middles sit at 5, 1 and 1 kW, so power barely separates them — a local-channel station may run as much power as a regional one and still be class C.

Two things look wrong here and are not. 191 FM stations are class D, running a median 34 watts and all but one of them under 100: a legacy educational class largely closed to new applicants, which is why they sit beside 100 kW neighbours reading as almost nothing. And a handful of AM records carry no class at all, which is why the legend promises only that a letter may follow the code.

Translators, boosters and why one station has several entries

A translator picks up a station and repeats it on a different frequency, which is why a small town can carry a city station down at the far end of the dial. It has its own call sign and its own place on the dial, so it is listed separately here — which is right, because that is where you tune for it.

A booster repeats its parent on the same frequency, filling a valley or a downtown shadow the main transmitter cannot reach. It is listed separately too, since its transmitter is somewhere else and that is the whole reason you can hear the station where you are. But it is not a separate station to listen to: it carries a derived call sign like KHPR-FM1 and identifies on air as its parent. Two entries on one frequency close together is usually a station and its booster, not two stations.

Licensed is not the same as on the air

A station may stay silent for up to twelve months and keep its licence. The FCC's data does not mark it, and neither can this — a station listed here may have been dark for months.

Across the borders

Canadian and Mexican FM stations appear, because the FCC records them under the coordination agreements that keep the two sides from interfering with each other. That coverage reaches a few hundred kilometres inland and no further, so a Vancouver or Monterrey listener is served and a Winnipeg or Mexico City one is not.

AM reaches very much further. Medium wave crosses the hemisphere after dark, so every country in the Americas notifies its assignments and the FCC files all of them — thirty-eight countries, some seven thousand stations from Mexico, Brazil, Cuba, Colombia and beyond. They are listed here. By day they are irrelevant to you; on a winter night they are the whole point.

What is missing

Formats and programming. The FCC licenses transmitters, not what they play, so nothing here knows which station is country and which is news — and format is the thing about a station most likely to change.
